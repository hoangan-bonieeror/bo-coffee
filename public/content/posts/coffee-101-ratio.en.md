> [!NOTE]
> **Key idea:** ratio decides whether a cup is **strong or weak**. Grind and time decide whether it is **under- or over-extracted**. Keeping those two apart makes dialling in much faster.

## 1. Reading a ratio

**1:16** means 1 gram of coffee to 16 grams of water. 15 g of coffee → 240 g of water. For water, 1 g ≈ 1 ml, so a kitchen scale is all you need.

Try it with the tool below — edit the coffee or water, or drag the ratio:

```brew-calc
method: v60
coffee: 15
ratio: 16
```

## 2. Why weigh instead of scoop

A scoop of dark roast weighs less than a scoop of light roast, because longer roasting dries the bean out and puffs it up. Weighing in grams is the same every time — and only what you can repeat can be improved.

## 3. Starting points by method

| Method | Suggested ratio | Notes |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Phin | 1:4 – 1:8 | A concentrate, usually with condensed milk |
| Espresso | 1:2 | By yield: 18 g in, 36 g out |
| Cold brew (concentrate) | 1:8 | Dilute to drink |
| AeroPress | 1:12 – 1:16 | Very flexible |
| French press | 1:12 – 1:17 | |
| V60, pour-over | 1:15 – 1:17 | |

For filter coffee, the SCA **Gold Cup** standard recommends about 55 g of coffee per litre of water, roughly 1:18; a cup that meets it has 18–22% extraction and 1.15–1.35% TDS ([Seattle Coffee Gear](https://www.seattlecoffeegear.com/blogs/learning-center/achieving-the-gold-cup-standard-your-guide-to-brewing-excellence)). Most home pour-over recipes run a little stronger, around 1:15–1:17.

## 4. Stronger or lighter

> [!TIP]
> **Tasty but weak** → lower the second number (1:16 → 1:15).
> **Tasty but heavy** → raise it (1:16 → 1:17).
> **Sour or bitter** → keep the ratio and adjust the **grind** first.
