> [!NOTE]
> **Key idea:** tasting isn't a talent. It's the habit of asking the same few questions of every cup, and writing the answers down.

## 1. Four questions for every cup

1. **Acidity:** bright and fresh like orange or apple — or sharp like vinegar? *Pleasant* acidity is a plus.
2. **Sweetness:** does it feel sweet, like caramel or honey, without any sugar?
3. **Bitterness:** pleasant like dark chocolate, or burnt and harsh?
4. **Finish:** after you swallow, how long does the taste linger, and is it pleasant or drying?

Add one more if you like: **body** — does the cup feel thin like tea or round like milk?

## 2. How to taste

- Let the coffee cool a little. Around 50–60 °C, sweetness and acidity show far more than when it is scalding.
- Slurp a small sip hard so it spreads across your tongue and up to your nose — smell makes up much of "taste".
- Compare two cups side by side. Differences jump out far more than when tasting one cup alone.

## 3. Naming flavors

The hardest part is finding words. The wheel below groups notes into eight families. Start from the inner ring ("fruity", "nutty & cocoa"…) before moving outward. Tap a family to read about it.

> [!TIP]
> **Herbal** notes or sharp sourness often signal under-extraction; heavy **Roasted** notes (smoky, burnt) often mean an over-roast or over-extraction.

## 4. Write it down

When you save a brew to the **Journal**, you pick flavor notes right on this wheel. After a few dozen cups, the Journal shows which notes keep appearing in the cups you rated 4–5 stars — your actual taste.
